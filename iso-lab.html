ISO Noise Lab

Why does lower ISO look cleaner?

Because lowering ISO makes you, or the camera, give the sensor more light to keep the picture bright. The extra light is what removes the noise. ISO only sets how much the signal is amplified after the light has been captured.

So you are right that the picture gets cleaner, and the people telling you ISO doesn't cause noise are right too. Try both below.

What ISO does in the signal chain

A sensor's sensitivity is fixed when it is manufactured. Nothing on the camera body changes how many photons each pixel catches. ISO sits one step later.

  1. Light arrives

    Set by your lights, the aperture and the shutter. Light is grainy by nature. Count N photons and the noise is √N, so four times the light gives twice the cleanliness.

  2. Pixels count it

    Each pixel turns photons into electrons until its well is full. A little electronic noise is added as the pixel is read.

  3. Analog gain

    Multiplies the signal and all the noise in it by the same factor. This is the ISO dial.

  4. Converter

    Turns the voltage into numbers. Anything above its ceiling clips to white. Adds a fixed amount of noise after the gain. Large on older cameras, small on new ones.

  5. Processing

    Tone curve, noise reduction, compression. Extended ISO settings are plain digital brightening done here.

Why your studio cameras make it so obvious

  • Lowering ISO forces more light. The picture goes dark, so you add lights or open the aperture, or auto exposure does it for you. That is step 1 changing, and step 1 is where most noise comes from.
  • Small sensors collect less light. At the same f-stop and framing a small sensor gathers a fraction of what a full-frame one does, so it starts out noisier and every stop of light matters more.
  • Low ISO leaves room for more light. At base ISO the pixels can fill completely before clipping. That is the real advantage of low ISO: it lets you pour in the most light.
  • Cheap cameras hide the trade. Webcams and auto modes raise gain silently when light is short, and heavy noise reduction smears what is left. Adding light makes the camera drop its own gain.

What to do with this

  • Treat light as the noise control. Add light, open up, or slow the shutter first, then set ISO to whatever makes the picture the right brightness.
  • For video the shutter is usually pinned near 1/50, so lights and aperture are the only real levers.
  • Don't shoot dark at low ISO and lift it in the edit. On older cameras that is worse than using the higher ISO, as the second experiment shows.
  • When light is fixed and short, a higher ISO costs highlight headroom, and little else.
  • On a dual native ISO camera, switch to the higher base when you are short on light. It is cleaner than the settings just below it.

How this fits the notes in this vault

ISO 12232 defines a digital camera's ISO by how bright the finished image comes out for a given exposure, which is why the notes found it so arbitrary. It is a label that tells you how much light to supply. On film, a faster stock was a physically different, grainier emulsion, and that is where the habit of blaming the ISO number for noise comes from.

The simulator is a simplified model: photon shot noise, read noise before and after the gain stage, and clipping at the converter. The camera numbers are representative of their class and are not measurements of any specific model. Real cameras also apply noise reduction and compression, which this leaves out.
